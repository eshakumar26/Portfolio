Shoulder-surf-proof keypad

Enter the same PIN twice and see what a person watching over your shoulder would learn. Turn the shuffle on and off to compare. This is a simplified, interactive illustration of the idea behind my published research on graphical password security.

1. Enter the PIN

Press the digits of your PIN in order. Your PIN stays the same each attempt, so you can compare.

Your PIN

2. What an observer learns

Each row shows where on the screen the fingers tapped, in order. If two attempts match, a watcher can copy your PIN.

Finish an attempt to see it here.

How it works

  • Shuffling. After every press the digits move to new positions. The taps for the same PIN land in different places each time, so watching your fingers teaches an observer very little.
  • Cipher layer. The "Seen on the wire" line applies a one-time key to every digit (digit + random key, mod 10), a decimal version of a Vernam cipher. A fresh random key per attempt means the transmitted sequence also changes every time.
  • Randomness. Shuffles and keys come from the browser's cryptographic random number generator, because a Vernam cipher is only as strong as its key.
Honest note: this is a simplified illustration, not the prototype from the paper. Shuffling alone does not stop a camera that records both the screen and the tap, which is why the research pairs it with a cipher layer.
